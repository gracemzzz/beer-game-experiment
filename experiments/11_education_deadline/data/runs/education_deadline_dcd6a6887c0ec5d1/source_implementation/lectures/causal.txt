Supply chains connect several organizations whose decisions affect one another over time. A retailer serves customers and requests replenishment from a wholesaler. The wholesaler requests replenishment from a distributor, and the distributor requests production from a factory. Each organization sees its immediate transactions, while material takes time to move between positions.

A request and a delivery are different events. After a request is submitted, the requested material can still be awaiting dispatch or travelling. It has not yet become usable inventory. Shipments already travelling will arrive later, and upstream shortages can delay material that has not been dispatched. These delays mean that the consequences of an earlier decision may remain invisible in current inventory for several rounds.

Demand, orders, shipments, inventory, and backlog describe different parts of this process. A change in customer demand can alter the retailer's requests. Subsequent organizations respond to the requests they receive, and those responses can influence still later requests. Consequently, fluctuations in requests can become larger as they move upstream, even when the original customer fluctuation was modest. This amplification is called the bullwhip effect.

Shared inventory displays reveal conditions at other positions, but do not remove transport delays or make earlier decisions disappear. A causal account connects events across rounds: a request can precede dispatch, dispatch precedes arrival, and arrival changes what can be shipped. Understanding those connections explains how individually motivated actions can combine into changing chain conditions. This reading supplies an explanation of the system, without specifying an ordering formula, target inventory, or recommended quantity.
