Theoretical Background - The Bullwhip Effect
A small and relatively stable change in consumer demand at the retail level becomes increasingly distorted and
exaggerated as it travels upward through the chain, producing large swings in inventory and ordering behavior that bear
little resemblance to actual end-customer demand (Lee et al., 1997).
Demand Forecasting Errors
Statistical methods amplify noise (Chen et al., 2000; Lee et al., 1997)
Order Batching
Fixed transaction costs create lumpy orders (Cachon & Fisher, 2000; Lee
et al., 1997)
Price Speculation
Forward-buying distorts true demand signals (Cachon et al., 2007)
Rationing Behavior
Artificial shortages trigger panic ordering (Cachon & Lariviere, 1999)

Literature Review
Selected Literatures
Behavioral Causes of Supply-Chain Ordering Decisions
Inventory sharing partially reduces the bullwhip effect, but supply-line underweighting persists (Croson and Donohue, 2006).
Abstract problem solving improves performance under limited information; its advantage becomes negligible under complete information (Cantor
and Macdonald, 2009).
Cognitive reflection is associated with how participants account for outstanding orders (Narayanan and Moritz, 2015).
Decision-Making under Time Pressure
Mental Models and Learned Routines
Decision makers adjust their processing strategies to time constraints and the opportunity cost of deliberation, balancing accuracy against
cognitive effort (Payne et al., 1988, 1996).
High time pressure can increase reliance on selected cues and experiential responses. Effort-reduction theory explains how heuristics simplify
information processing (Rieskamp and Hoffrage, 2008; Shah and Oppenheimer, 2008; Fraser-Mackenzie and Dror, 2011).
More accurate understanding of key system principles is associated with better decision rules and performance. How knowledge is represented
also shapes interpretation and decision-making (Boland et al., 2001; Gary and Wood, 2011).
Established routines can persist after conditions change. Severe time pressure can also increase returns to previously practiced responses
despite intentions to change (Betsch et al., 2001, 2004).

Hypotheses
RQ#1
How does a binding decision-time constraint affect the bullwhip effects and supply
chain performance?
H1
H2
Within an information-sharing environment, a binding decision-time constraint increases the
underweighting of outstanding replenishment orders relative to adequate decision time.
Supply chains operating under a binding decision-time constraint incur higher cumulative
total inventory-holding and backlog costs than supply chains operating with adequate
decision time.
Level of measurement: individual participant
Level of measurement: entire supply chain

H3
H4
Under a binding decision-time constraint, participants receiving causal education about
supply-chain dynamics exhibit less underweighting of outstanding replenishment orders
than participants receiving a matched control lecture.
Under a binding decision-time constraint, supply chains receiving causal education about
supply-chain dynamics incur lower (a) individual order variation and (b) bullwhip ratio
