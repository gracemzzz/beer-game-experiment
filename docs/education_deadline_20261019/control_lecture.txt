Supply chains connect several organizations with distinct names and responsibilities. A retailer serves final customers. A wholesaler supplies retailers, a distributor supplies wholesalers, and a factory makes the product. Labels identify the organizations represented by the boxes, and arrows identify the direction in which a transaction is recorded.

Business records use conventional units. A case is a unit of product, a week is a unit of time, and a dollar is a unit of money. Tables usually place observations in rows and named variables in columns. A column heading identifies what its entries describe. A caption explains the setting of a table or chart, while a legend identifies the visual marks used in a figure.

A line chart uses an ordered horizontal axis and a numerical vertical axis. Different lines can identify different organizations. A table can instead show a separate column for each organization. These are alternative ways of presenting recorded observations. Consistent labels help a reader identify which organization, period, and unit an entry describes. Titles distinguish one record from another without changing the recorded values.

Classroom business exercises often assign roles, provide written instructions, and ask participants to submit decisions through a form. The submitted entries are stored as records for later discussion. Researchers can organize those records using role names and period labels, and can describe the exercise in a written report. This reading introduces terminology and presentation conventions. It does not specify an ordering formula, target inventory, recommended quantity, or explanation of how ordering decisions affect later supply-chain conditions.
